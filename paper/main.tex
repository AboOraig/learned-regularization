\begin{abstract}
We study sparse-view computed tomography as a linear inverse problem and compare classical
variational regularization against a learned image prior used inside a Plug-and-Play
proximal-gradient (PnP-PGD) scheme, benchmarking it against filtered back-projection, Tikhonov and
total-variation (TV) regularization. We use disjoint train/validation/test splits, per-condition
hyperparameter tuning, paired statistical tests, measurements simulated on a finer grid to avoid the
inverse crime, and three independently trained denoisers per configuration. The learned prior improves
PSNR by up to 4\,dB over TV at moderate-to-high noise ($\sigma\ge0.05$) and for 5--90 projection angles;
in the near noise-free regime TV remains ahead, by an amount (1--6\,dB) that depends on the forward-model
mismatch of the simulated data. Reconstruction quality peaks after a handful of iterations and then
degrades while the data residual rises. The advantage narrows under forward-model mismatch and reverses
under distribution shift: a prior trained on MNIST loses to TV by 3--4\,dB on Fashion-MNIST and on a
Shepp--Logan phantom, whereas a prior trained on Fashion-MNIST beats TV on Fashion-MNIST by 2.2\,dB, so
the failure is one of prior mismatch rather than of the PnP scheme itself. An error decomposition along
the singular vectors of the forward operator shows that, under mismatch, the reconstruction places up to
three times the true energy in the weakly determined subspace. Finally, lesion-insertion experiments show
that even in distribution the PSNR-tuned PnP reconstruction detects small inserted lesions less reliably
than TV (paired AUC $0.69$ vs.\ $0.91$ for a $0.75$\,px lesion), attenuating them rather than inventing
structure, whereas under prior mismatch it creates lesion-like blobs up to an order of magnitude more often
than TV. Re-tuning towards lesion fidelity recovers TV-level detection only at the price of its PSNR advantage
or of false structure. We argue that task-based and robustness evaluation should be standard components of
learned-prior reconstruction studies.
We then replace the explicit forward matrix by a matrix-free operator with an exactly matched adjoint and
the Gaussian noise by a Beer--Lambert Poisson model with 10--1000 photons per ray; the ranking of the methods,
the reversal under distribution shift and the lesion results are unchanged, so they do not depend on a
dense operator or on additive Gaussian noise.




\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Image reconstruction from indirect, noisy, and incomplete measurements
is a classical ill-posed inverse problem, arising in computed
tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging, and many other imaging
modalities. Given a forward operator $A$ and noisy measurements
\begin{equation}
y = Ax + n,
\end{equation}
the goal is to recover $x$ from $y$ when $A$ is ill-conditioned or
rank-deficient, as is the case for sparse-view CT with few projection
angles. Classical approaches address ill-posedness through explicit
regularization, solving
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert Ax - y \rVert_2^2 + \lambda R(x),
\end{equation}
with $R$ a hand-crafted prior such as Tikhonov (L2) or total variation
(TV) penalties. In recent years, learned image priors -- most notably
through Plug-and-Play (PnP) methods and deep unrolling -- have been
proposed to replace or augment hand-crafted regularizers with a
denoising neural network, exploiting the empirical observation that
image denoisers implicitly encode rich natural-image statistics
\citep{venkatakrishnan2013plugandplay}.

While learned priors frequently outperform classical regularization on
in-distribution benchmarks, a growing body of work has raised concerns
about their \emph{robustness}: sensitivity to distribution shift, lack
of convergence guarantees, and instabilities under small perturbations
of the input or forward model \citep{antun2020instabilities}. Many
project-level or introductory studies of learned reconstruction report
only in-distribution accuracy, leaving these robustness questions
unaddressed.

This paper documents a controlled, small-scale study designed
explicitly to probe these questions. We deliberately restrict scope
to a simple, cheaply reproducible setting -- sparse-view tomography of
$28\times28$ MNIST-derived images -- in order to run a thorough grid
of experiments with proper train/validation/test protocol rather than
a single headline comparison. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A from-scratch implementation of a sparse-view Radon operator
with exact adjoint, classical regularization baselines (FBP, Tikhonov,
TV-ADMM), and a PnP-PGD solver using a noise-conditioned DnCNN prior.
\item A systematic comparison of PSNR/SSIM accuracy across measurement
noise levels and numbers of projection angles, with validation-tuned
hyperparameters and paired significance testing.
\item A convergence analysis explaining why PnP-PGD selects very few
iterations under validation tuning, showing a peak-then-decay pattern
in reconstruction accuracy that is not visible from residual curves
alone.
\item Robustness studies -- forward-model mismatch (angle offset
and jitter) and train-test distribution shift (Fashion-MNIST, EMNIST,
and a CT phantom) -- run with hyperparameters frozen at their nominal
values, exposing failure modes not visible under in-distribution
evaluation alone.
\item Data simulated on a finer grid than the reconstruction grid, to remove the inverse crime, with a sensitivity analysis over how the finer-grid object is constructed (Section~\ref{sec:crime-results}).
\item An error decomposition along the singular vectors of the forward operator, separating the weakly determined subspace from its orthogonal complement, with an ``injection'' and an ``alignment'' statistic that quantify prior-driven content (Section~\ref{sec:nullspace-results}).
\item Controls for the learned prior itself: three denoiser seeds, a wider training noise range, and a  matched-prior control trained on the shifted distribution (Section~\ref{sec:seeds-results}).
\item A task-based evaluation by lesion insertion, with an operating-point study asking whether the detectability loss can be removed by re-tuning (Sections~\ref{sec:lesion-results} and \ref{sec:oppoint-results}).
\item A matrix-free implementation of the projector and its exact adjoint, validated against the dense operator, and a
      Poisson (low-dose) noise study, showing that the conclusions above carry over to the settings required for
      clinical-size data (Sections~\ref{sec:mf-results}--\ref{sec:lesion-poisson}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Variational regularization.} Tikhonov regularization and
total variation \citep{rudin1992tv} remain standard baselines for
ill-posed inverse problems due to their convexity, well-understood
convergence properties, and interpretability. TV in particular is a
strong prior for piecewise-constant images, which makes it a
competitive baseline for digit-like data such as MNIST.

\paragraph{Plug-and-Play priors.}
\citet{venkatakrishnan2013plugandplay} introduced the idea of
replacing the proximal operator of a variational regularizer with an
off-the-shelf denoiser inside an ADMM or proximal-gradient iteration.
Subsequent work analyzed convergence conditions for PnP under
assumptions on the denoiser (e.g. non-expansiveness or bounded
Lipschitz constant) \citep{chan2016pnp,ryu2019pnp}; without such
constraints, plain CNN denoisers offer no convergence guarantee, a
point directly relevant to the convergence behavior we observe in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{Learned denoisers.} DnCNN \citep{zhang2017dncnn}
demonstrated that residual CNNs trained for Gaussian denoising across
a range of noise levels generalize well as plug-in priors. We adopt
this noise-conditioning strategy so that a single trained network can
serve across the PnP iteration trajectory, where the effective
denoising strength is a tunable hyperparameter.

\paragraph{Instabilities and robustness.} \citet{antun2020instabilities}
showed that learned reconstruction methods for CT and MRI can be
unstable to small perturbations and structural changes not present in
training data, in some cases more so than classical methods. Related
work on distribution shift in learned inverse problem solvers
\citep{darestani2021shift} similarly finds that accuracy gains from
learned priors can shrink or reverse under shift. Our distribution
shift and forward-model mismatch experiments are a small-scale,
project-level instantiation of this broader concern, and our findings
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) are consistent with it.

\paragraph{Hallucinations, the inverse crime and task-based assessment.}
Bhadra et al.~\cite{bhadra2021} define a hallucination in tomographic reconstruction as image content
that is not determined by the measurement data, i.e.\ content that lives in the null space of the
imaging operator and is supplied by the prior; we adopt this view and extend it to the weakly determined
subspace of a finite-precision operator (Section~\ref{sec:subspace}). Generating data with the same discrete
model that is later inverted is known as the inverse crime~\cite{wirgin2004,kaipio2005} and tends to
overstate the accuracy of methods that exploit the exact model; we remove it in Section~\ref{sec:crimefree}.
Finally, we follow the task-based view of image quality~\cite{barrett2004}: fidelity metrics such as PSNR
need not predict whether a clinically relevant feature survives reconstruction, so we also measure
detection of inserted lesions.




\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Forward operator}
We construct a sparse-view Radon transform $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$
as an explicit dense matrix by applying the discrete Radon transform to
each canonical basis image and stacking the resulting sinogram columns.
This gives an exact adjoint $A^\top$ (rather than an approximate one,
as is common with implicit/fast Radon implementations), which is
important for the PnP gradient step, and allows the reconstruction
operator to be perturbed independently of the data-generating operator
for the mismatch experiments in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
Images are $28\times28$; the number of projection angles and, where
relevant, an angular offset or per-angle jitter are configurable.

\subsection{Classical baselines}
\textbf{Filtered back-projection (FBP)} applies the standard ramp
filter and inverse Radon transform. \textbf{Tikhonov regularization}
solves
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert Ax-y\rVert_2^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert x\rVert_2^2
= (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1} A^\top y,
\end{equation}
computed in closed form. \textbf{Total variation} solves
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert Ax - y\rVert_2^2 + \lambda \, \mathrm{TV}(x)
\end{equation}
via ADMM with an isotropic TV penalty and a finite-difference operator
$D$, splitting $z = Dx$ and applying the standard shrinkage update on
the split variable.

\subsection{Learned prior: PnP proximal gradient}
We train a DnCNN denoiser $D_\sigma$ conditioned on the noise level
$\sigma$ (following the FFDNet-style noise-map input), on clean MNIST
digits corrupted with Gaussian noise $\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}(0,\sigma_{\max})$,
$\sigma_{\max}=0.3$, using residual learning and an $\ell_2$ loss.
A single trained network is then used across all PnP experiments,
with $\sigma_d$ (the denoising strength argument passed at inference)
treated as a tunable hyperparameter distinct from the training
distribution's noise range.

Reconstruction proceeds by proximal-gradient iteration, replacing the
proximal operator of an explicit regularizer with the learned
denoiser:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = D_{\sigma_d}\big(x_k - \eta \, A^\top(Ax_k - y)\big), \qquad
\eta = 1/L(A^\top A),
\end{equation}
where $L(A^\top A)$ is the largest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$, estimated
by power iteration. The iteration is initialized at the FBP
reconstruction. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pnp} summarizes the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{PnP proximal gradient (PnP-PGD)}
\label{alg:pnp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require measurements $y$, operator $A$, denoiser $D$, strength $\sigma_d$, step $\eta$, iterations $K$
\State $x_0 \gets \text{FBP}(y)$
\For{$k = 0, \dots, K-1$}
  \State $g_k \gets A^\top(Ax_k - y)$
  \State $x_{k+1} \gets D_{\sigma_d}(x_k - \eta \, g_k)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $x_K$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
Data are split into disjoint training, validation, and test subsets.
All hyperparameters -- $\lambda$ for Tikhonov and TV, and
$(\sigma_d, K)$ for PnP -- are selected by grid search maximizing mean
validation PSNR, independently for each experimental condition
(noise level or number of angles) in Experiments~1--2, and then
evaluated once on the disjoint test set. For the robustness
experiments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, E3--E4), hyperparameters
are tuned once at a single nominal condition and then \emph{frozen}
across all mismatch/shift conditions, reflecting a realistic
deployment scenario where a reconstruction pipeline is not retuned
for every possible operating condition. We report PSNR and SSIM as
mean with 95\% confidence intervals over test images, and use a
paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (matched test images and noise
realizations) to assess the significance of PnP vs.\ TV differences.


\subsection{Data simulated on a finer grid}\label{sec:crimefree}
In E1--E5 the data are generated with the matrix $A$ used for reconstruction (an ``inverse
crime''~\cite{wirgin2004,kaipio2005}). For the experiments of Sections~\ref{sec:crime-results}--\ref{sec:oppoint-results}
we instead simulate measurements on a finer grid. The $28\times28$ image $x$ is upsampled by an odd integer
factor $f=5$ (to $140\times140$) with bicubic interpolation and clipped to $[0,1]$, projected with the same
discrete Radon transform at the same angles, and every $f$ adjacent detector bins are summed onto the 40-bin
detector of the reconstruction operator, giving $y=\mathcal{S}(x)+n$ with $\mathcal{S}\neq A$. Reconstructions use
$A$, and all metrics use the original $28\times28$ image as ground truth. The model error
$\|\mathcal{S}(x)-Ax\|_2$ averages $0.09$ at 20 angles ($0.05$ at 5 and $0.17$ at 90 angles), i.e.\ 6\% of the
noise norm $\sigma\sqrt{m}$, $m=40\,n_{\rm angles}$, at $\sigma=0.05$ and 31\% at $\sigma=0.01$. Because bicubic
upsampling smooths the object, we also report a nearest-neighbour variant (object unchanged, model error $0.03$) and
a bilinear variant (model error $\approx0.10$, a stronger smoothing) as a sensitivity band. All hyperparameters are
re-tuned on validation data simulated in the same way.
 
\subsection{Weakly determined subspaces and prior-driven content}\label{sec:subspace}
Let $A=U\Sigma V^{\top}$ be the SVD of the dense operator, with singular values $s_1\ge\dots\ge s_n$. For a
threshold $\tau$ let $W_\tau=\mathrm{span}\{v_i : s_i<\tau s_1\}$ and let $P_\tau$ be the orthogonal projector onto
$W_\tau$. For 20 angles the operator has an exact null space of dimension 79 (singular values at machine precision),
followed by a continuum of very small singular values with no gap; we therefore use $\tau=0.05$ as the headline
choice, giving $\dim W_{0.05}=319$ of the 784 dimensions (and report $\tau=0.10$ and the exact null space as
sensitivity checks). A gradient step $x\mapsto x-\eta A^\top(Ax-y)$ never changes the component of $x$ in the null
space of $A$, so null-space content of a PnP reconstruction is supplied by the denoiser alone. For a reconstruction
$\hat x$ and ground truth $x$ we report
\begin{align}
\|\hat x-x\|_2^2 &= \|P_\tau(\hat x-x)\|_2^2+\|(I-P_\tau)(\hat x-x)\|_2^2 \quad\text{(orthogonal split)},\\
\iota &= \frac{\sum\|P_\tau\hat x\|_2^2}{\sum\|P_\tau x\|_2^2}\quad\text{(injection)},\\
c &= \frac{\sum\langle P_\tau\hat x,P_\tau x\rangle}{\sqrt{\sum\|P_\tau\hat x\|_2^2\,\sum\|P_\tau x\|_2^2}}\quad\text{(alignment)},
\end{align}
with sums over test images (pooled ratios). $\iota>1$ means that the reconstruction places more energy in
$W_\tau$ than the true images contain there; $c$ measures how much of that content points in the right direction.
These statistics cannot by themselves distinguish prior-driven structure from amplified noise, so we interpret them
only in comparisons with identical noise, data and solver settings.
 
\subsection{Denoiser seeds, noise range and matched-prior control}\label{sec:seeds}
Besides the single MNIST denoiser of Section~3.3 we train, with the same architecture and
optimiser (8 epochs, Adam, cosine schedule): (i) three MNIST denoisers with $\sigma_{\max}=0.3$ (seeds 0--2);
(ii) three MNIST denoisers with $\sigma_{\max}=0.5$, which allows $\sigma_d$ up to $0.5$ and removes the grid-edge
tuning at $\sigma=0.2$ observed in E1; (iii) three denoisers trained on the Fashion-MNIST training split
($\sigma_{\max}=0.3$). In the matched-prior control, all methods (Tikhonov, TV, PnP) are tuned on held-out
Fashion-MNIST training images and tested on the Fashion-MNIST test split. The $\sigma_d$ grid is extended to
$\{0.005,\dots,0.3,0.4,0.5\}$ for all denoisers.
 
\subsection{Lesion-insertion protocol}\label{sec:lesion}
A lesion is an additive Gaussian blob, $x_{\rm les}=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\bigl(x+c\,e^{-\|p-p_0\|^2/(2\sigma_l^2)}\bigr)$,
inserted at a random integer location $p_0$ of the central $16\times16$ region of a test image, with
$\sigma_l\in\{0.5,0.75,1.25,2.0\}$\,px and contrast $c\in\{0.25,0.5\}$ (eight lesion types). We reconstruct the
lesion-present image and the lesion-absent original with independent noise realizations ($\sigma=0.05$, 20 angles,
crime-free data), using the same locations for every method. A lesion is \emph{valid} if its inserted contrast in the
ground truth is at least 50\% of nominal (a lesion placed on a saturated stroke is clipped away); invalid cases are
excluded for all methods, leaving 157--197 of 200 images per type. The observer is a location-known
statistic, the \emph{local contrast}: the mean of the image in a disc of radius $\rho=\lceil1.5\sigma_l\rceil$ minus its
mean in the surrounding annulus of width 3 px. We report
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{paired AUC}: the fraction of images for which the statistic is larger in the lesion-present than in the
      lesion-absent reconstruction of the same base image (the background cancels; ties count one half);
\item the \emph{retention} $R=\mathrm{E}[t(\hat x_{\rm present})-t(\hat x_{\rm absent})]/\mathrm{E}[t(x_{\rm les})-t(x)]$,
      which is $1$ if the lesion contrast is preserved and below 1 if it is attenuated;
\item the number of \emph{false lesions} per lesion-absent image: local maxima of the contrast map of the
      reconstruction whose response is at least $\theta$, half the response of the nominal lesion, and for which the
      ground truth has no contrast above $\theta/2$ within 2\,px;
\item the lesion's \emph{observability}, the fraction of its energy outside $W_{0.05}$.
\end{itemize}
For the operating-point study (Section~\ref{sec:oppoint-results}) we sweep 45 PnP settings ($\sigma_d\in\{0.02,0.05,0.1,0.15,0.2\}$,
$K\in\{1,2,3,5,10,25,50,100,200\}$) and 9 values of the TV weight, choose operating points on 200 held-out
\emph{in-distribution} validation images (disjoint from the images used for PSNR tuning) and report them on the
test sets, so that the choice is neither made on the evaluation images nor informed by the shifted distribution.


\subsection{Matrix-free operator}\label{sec:mf}
Up to this point the forward operator $A$ was stored as a dense $800\times784$ matrix, which allows exact adjoints,
closed-form Tikhonov inverses and a full singular value decomposition, but does not scale to clinical image sizes. We
therefore implemented a matrix-free operator that is never assembled. The forward projection embeds the image in a
$40\times40$ canvas (the diagonal of the image, as in the dense operator), rotates it by each angle with bilinear
interpolation about the pixel used by the reference implementation, and sums along the detector rows. The adjoint is the
exact transpose of this discretisation, obtained by reverse-mode differentiation, so $\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^{\top}y\rangle$
holds to floating-point precision by construction rather than approximately. The classical baselines use only forward
and adjoint applications: Tikhonov by conjugate gradients on $A^{\top}A+\lambda I$, and TV by the same ADMM as before with the $x$-update solved by
conjugate gradients. The singular subspaces used in Section~\ref{sec:subspace} are obtained with a randomized SVD
\cite{halko2011} (power iterations and oversampling, forward and adjoint products only); the error split in
Section~\ref{sec:nullspace-results} then uses the well-determined subspace $\{i: s_i/s_1\ge0.05\}$ and its complement.
 
\subsection{Poisson noise}\label{sec:poisson}
Low-dose CT measures photon counts. With $p=Ax$ the (normalised) line integrals, the expected counts are
$I=I_0\exp(-\mu p)$ with $I_0$ incident photons per ray and attenuation scale $\mu=2$; the counts are
$N\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(I)$ and the data used for reconstruction are $\hat p=-\mu^{-1}\log(\max(N,1)/I_0)$.
The noise in $\hat p$ is signal dependent, with variance approximately $e^{\mu p}/(I_0\mu^2)$, and is larger along
rays that pass through more material. We use $I_0\in\{1000,300,100,30,10\}$, which gives an effective noise
standard deviation of $0.018,0.032,0.056,0.106,0.198$ on the sinogram and therefore spans the Gaussian range
$\sigma\in[0.02,0.2]$ of the earlier experiments. The data are generated from the finer-grid object
(Section~\ref{sec:crimefree}, bicubic, factor 5), so the inverse crime remains removed. The reconstruction methods are
unchanged and treat $\hat p$ with an unweighted least-squares data term (a Gaussian approximation); for PnP we also
tested a variant with weights $w=N/I_0$ (an inverse-variance weighting up to a constant). All hyperparameters are tuned at each dose on 100 held-out MNIST validation images
and frozen for every test set.

\subsection{E5: Why PnP selects few iterations}
Validation tuning consistently selects a small number of PnP
iterations (5--25 for $\ge 10$ angles and $\sigma\le 0.2$, and 100--400 for the 5-angle condition). Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}
shows PSNR and the data-fidelity residual $\lVert Ax_k - y\rVert$ as a
function of iteration count, for several values of $\sigma_d$, on 50
test images. PSNR rises rapidly and peaks within the first 5--15
iterations, then \emph{decays} to a lower plateau (e.g.\ $\sigma_d=0.1$:
peak $\approx24.0$\,dB near iteration 10, plateau $\approx22.0$\,dB by
iteration 400). Over the same range, the residual does not decrease;
it rises and plateaus, more steeply for larger $\sigma_d$. This
indicates that the iteration does not converge to a fixed point that
is simultaneously data-consistent and maximally accurate: the
denoiser's implicit prior increasingly dominates the data-fidelity
term as iterations progress, without a corresponding gain in
accuracy. Early stopping is therefore not an artifact of a
coarse hyperparameter grid but an active, necessary part of the
regularization.

\subsection{E7: Error decomposition along the singular vectors}\label{sec:nullspace-results}
Table~\ref{tab:nullspace} reports the decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:subspace} at $\sigma=0.05$ and 20 angles
($\dim W_{0.05}=319$). In distribution, PnP has about 0.4--0.5 times TV's error energy in \emph{both} the weakly
determined subspace and its complement (null-space ratio 0.48, range-space ratio 0.40) and a better-aligned
completion of the unobserved directions ($c=0.76$ versus $0.45$): the prior's advantage is not confined to
filling in unobserved directions. Under shift both ratios exceed 1 on Fashion-MNIST (1.27 and 2.32) and on the
phantom (2.59 and 2.64), so the failure is not confined to the weakly determined subspace either, and the largest part of
the excess error lies in its complement. The injection statistic shows the hallucination signature: on the phantom PnP
places $3.3$ times the true energy in $W_{0.05}$ (TV: $0.44$) with alignment $0.23$, while on MNIST its $0.76$ comes with
alignment $0.76$. Within the iteration, the contraction of direction $i$ by the gradient step after $K$ iterations is
$(1-(s_i/s_1)^2)^K$, so at $K=5$ a direction with $s_i=0.1\,s_1$ retains 95\% of its initial error, $s_i=0.3\,s_1$ 62\%
and $s_i=0.5\,s_1$ 24\%: the tuned iteration leaves a large share of directions to the denoiser. On the shifted sets
no setting of $(\sigma_d,K)$ recovers TV's accuracy: the best oracle setting still has $1.5$--$1.9$ times TV's error
and the error grows with $K$ (to $3.7$--$4.6$ times at $K=400$; bilinear fine-grid data).

\subsection{E8: Denoiser seeds and matched-prior control}\label{sec:seeds-results}
Table~\ref{tab:seeds} reports the shift experiment with three denoisers per configuration (bicubic data, $\sigma=0.05$,
20 angles; all methods re-tuned on the validation distribution of the respective prior). Seed-to-seed variation is at
most 0.3\,dB, an order of magnitude below the effects of interest: on Fashion-MNIST the MNIST-trained prior is
$3.10\pm0.30$\,dB below TV (range $-3.43$ to $-2.83$) and on the phantom $4.33\pm0.12$\,dB below. Training with
$\sigma_{\max}=0.5$ instead of $0.3$ does not change these results (Fashion-MNIST $-3.12$, phantom $-3.94$\,dB) and,
at $\sigma=0.2$, the tuned $\sigma_d$ is $0.3$ for all six MNIST denoisers although values up to $0.5$ were available, so
the grid-edge tuning in E1 was not an artefact; the wider-range denoiser is slightly better at high noise
($+1.44\pm0.11$ versus $+0.97\pm0.11$\,dB over TV at $\sigma=0.2$, ranges not overlapping) and slightly worse at
$\sigma=0.05$.
 
The matched-prior control isolates the cause of the reversal: a prior trained on Fashion-MNIST beats TV on
Fashion-MNIST by $+2.21\pm0.01$\,dB (error ratio $0.63$), although less than the MNIST prior on MNIST ($+3.84$\,dB),
since Fashion-MNIST images are less sparse. The control is clean: TV's PSNR is identical for all priors on every test
set, i.e.\ tuning on Fashion-MNIST validation data selects the same TV weight. Transfer is asymmetric: the Fashion-trained
prior still beats TV on MNIST ($+1.38$\,dB) and ties it on the phantom ($-0.18$\,dB, error ratio $1.05$), whereas the
MNIST-trained prior loses by $4.3$\,dB on the phantom. The injection statistic on the phantom follows the prior: $2.8$--$3.4$
for MNIST-trained denoisers versus $0.30$--$0.35$ for Fashion-trained ones, with identical noise and solver settings. Whether
the broader prior helps because it is richer or because Fashion-MNIST resembles the phantom more cannot be decided with two
training distributions.

\subsection{E9: Lesion insertion}\label{sec:lesion-results}
Table~\ref{tab:lesion} and Figure~\ref{fig:lesion-summary} summarise the lesion experiment (three denoisers per prior,
protocol of Section~\ref{sec:lesion}). Three observations hold across lesion types.
 
\emph{TV detects small lesions better than PnP, in distribution and for a matched prior.} For the MNIST-trained prior on MNIST
the paired AUC of PnP is $0.04$--$0.23$ below TV's (for example $0.69$ versus $0.91$ for $\sigma_l=0.75$, $c=0.5$), although
PnP is about $4$\,dB better in PSNR on the same images; for the matched Fashion-trained prior the deficit is $0.03$--$0.17$. The
two methods tie only for the largest high-contrast lesion. FBP and Tikhonov also detect small lesions better than PnP
(paired AUC $0.92$ at $\sigma_l=0.75$, $c=0.5$ on MNIST), at the cost of many false blobs (up to $6$ per image for FBP).
 
\emph{In distribution PnP attenuates lesions and invents none.} Its retention is $0.12$--$0.78$ for the MNIST prior on MNIST
and $0.16$--$0.49$ for the matched prior (TV: $0.29$--$0.88$ and $0.38$--$0.83$), and it produces at most $0.01$ false lesions per
image against up to $0.26$ for TV. The reconstructions are cleaner than TV's but the inserted lesion is often absent
(Figure~\ref{fig:lesion-examples}), so a visually convincing image does not imply a faithful one.
 
\emph{Under prior mismatch PnP invents structure.} For the MNIST-trained prior on Fashion-MNIST the retention is $0.78$--$1.04$ (the
lesion survives) but there are $0.20$--$0.66$ false lesions per image against $\le0.20$ for TV, and the paired AUC remains below
TV's by $0.05$--$0.16$. The matched Fashion-trained prior on the same images produces at most $0.01$. Lesion observability
(Section~\ref{sec:lesion}) does not explain these differences: the observability terciles formed within each lesion type span
only $0.87$--$0.95$ on average, and PnP's AUC does not increase with observability; smaller lesions are harder for all methods, but this is confounded with size.

\subsection{E10: Can re-tuning recover lesion detectability?}\label{sec:oppoint-results}
The PSNR-tuned operating point of PnP is not necessarily the best one for lesion detection, so we sweep operating points
(Section~\ref{sec:lesion}) and compare three rules for PnP---PSNR-tuned, best paired AUC on the selection set (``lesion''),
and best paired AUC among points whose selection-set PSNR is at least that of tuned TV (``constrained'')---with TV. Table~\ref{tab:oppoint}
and Figure~\ref{fig:oppoint} show one denoiser per prior. For the MNIST-trained prior in distribution, the constrained point keeps
a $+0.7$\,dB PSNR advantage over TV (the PSNR-tuned advantage was $+4.0$\,dB) but its paired AUC is still $0.06$ below TV's, and
its false-lesion rate rises to 12 times TV's; the lesion-tuned point matches TV's detection but has lower PSNR than TV and 60
times TV's false-lesion rate ($0.32$ versus $0.005$ per image). For the matched Fashion-trained prior the constrained point keeps
$+1.0$\,dB over TV, with a paired AUC within $0.02$--$0.03$ of TV's (about one standard error) and $0.085$ false lesions per image against
$0.03$ for TV. Under shift, operating points chosen in distribution do not protect: for the MNIST prior on Fashion-MNIST all three
rules are $2.1$--$2.8$\,dB below TV in PSNR and produce $0.40$--$0.51$ false lesions per image against $0.03$. Hence PSNR, detectability and
fabricated structure trade against each other, and only a matched prior leaves a point at which PnP is competitive with TV on all
three.

\subsection{E11: The matrix-free operator reproduces the dense pipeline}\label{sec:mf-results}
Table~\ref{tab:mf} compares the two implementations on the same 200 noisy test images ($\sigma=0.05$, 20 angles, bicubic
fine-grid data). The projections agree to $3\times10^{-7}$ in relative norm, and the three reconstruction methods agree to
$\le10^{-5}$ (Tikhonov, TV) and $3\times10^{-4}$ (PnP) with identical PSNR to three decimals. The randomized SVD
recovers the first 400 singular values to within $10^{-4}\,s_1$ and the same 465-dimensional well-determined subspace
(319 weakly determined directions) as the dense SVD, and the resulting strong/weak error split agrees with the dense one to within $1\%$ of the error norm.
At this size the dense implementation is much faster for the iterative solvers (Table~\ref{tab:mf}), since the adjoint is
evaluated by differentiation and TV needs about $3\times10^{3}$ forward--adjoint pairs per image; PnP is only 1.2 times slower
because the denoiser dominates its cost. The matrix-free operator is therefore a requirement for larger images, where the dense matrix cannot be stored, rather than an
optimisation at 28$\times$28.

\subsection{E12: Poisson dose sweep}\label{sec:dose-results}
Table~\ref{tab:dose} and Figure~\ref{fig:dose} give the PnP--TV difference over the dose range (200 test images per set; the
Shepp--Logan phantom is one image with one noise draw per dose, so it has no interval). The qualitative picture of
Stage~1 is reproduced.
 
\emph{Prior-matched data.} On MNIST the learned prior beats TV at every dose, by $+3.7$\,dB at $I_0=100$--$300$ (effective $\sigma\approx0.03$--$0.06$), against $+3.84$\,dB at Gaussian $\sigma=0.05$,
and for 99--100\% of the images at $I_0\ge100$. The advantage shrinks at both ends of the dose range, to $+0.8$\,dB at $I_0=10$, in line with the Gaussian result at $\sigma=0.2$
($+0.97$\,dB). EMNIST keeps a smaller advantage ($+0.6$ to $+1.8$\,dB).
 
\emph{Shifted data.} The MNIST-trained prior is below TV on Fashion-MNIST ($-2.0$ to $-3.1$\,dB; PnP wins on only 6--14\% of the images)
and on the phantom ($-4.4$ to $-6.1$\,dB), as in Stage~1 ($-3.10$ and $-4.33$\,dB). The share of the error in the complement of the well-determined subspace is $0.68$--$0.99$ of TV's on MNIST but $1.04$--$1.19$ on Fashion-MNIST
and $1.73$--$1.79$ on the phantom (for $I_0\le300$), so the mismatch error again sits partly in the weakly determined directions. These ratios are less favourable to PnP than the
Stage~1 values and are not directly comparable, since the noise model, the tuned settings and the subspace definition differ.
 
\emph{Weighted data term.} Using the Poisson weights in PnP gave no consistent benefit: the weighted variant was $0.14$--$0.59$\,dB below the unweighted one in 13 of the 15 non-phantom
cells, and above it in two (EMNIST and Fashion-MNIST at $I_0=300$), where the tuned $(\sigma_d,k)$ differed. We therefore report the unweighted scheme.

\subsection{E13: Lesion insertion under Poisson noise}\label{sec:lesion-poisson}
We repeated the lesion experiment (Section~\ref{sec:lesion}; same lesions, observer, locations and validity filter; three denoisers per prior, 200 images per set) with
Poisson data at $I_0=100$ and $I_0=30$ and the matrix-free operator, tuning each method on its prior's own validation distribution
(Table~\ref{tab:lesion-poisson}, Figure~\ref{fig:lesion-poisson}).
 
\emph{Erasure persists.} The paired AUC of PnP is below TV's in all four prior/test-set combinations and at both doses (differences of $-0.01$ to $-0.28$ at $I_0=100$, with one tie at $1.0$ for the largest high-contrast lesion, and $-0.02$ to $-0.19$ at $I_0=30$). For the MNIST prior on MNIST ($\sigma_l=0.75$, $c=0.5$) the paired AUC is $0.60$ against $0.89$ for TV and the retention is $0.29$ against $0.60$ at $I_0=100$,
which reproduces the Gaussian-noise values of Stage~1 ($0.69$ vs.\ $0.91$; $0.28$ vs.\ $0.59$), although PnP is $3.7$\,dB better in PSNR on the same images. At $I_0=30$ the gap narrows
($0.63$ vs.\ $0.77$) because TV degrades faster.
 
\emph{Invention under mismatch persists.} For the MNIST-trained prior on Fashion-MNIST, PnP produces more false lesions than TV for lesions with $\sigma_l\ge0.75$ in 11 of the 12 dose/lesion-type cells, by factors of $1.7$--$5.5$ ($0.25$--$0.70$ against $0.06$--$0.28$ per image at $I_0=100$; the exception is $0.25$ against $0.28$). The matched Fashion-trained prior, on its own data and on MNIST, stays at $\le0.09$ false lesions per image. Retention above that of TV
for the mismatched prior should not be read as fidelity, since invented contrast contributes to it.
 
\emph{New at the lower dose: in-distribution invention.} For the MNIST prior on MNIST at $I_0=30$, PnP produces $0.058$--$0.073$ false lesions per image for the four lesion types with $0.75\le\sigma_l\le1.25$, against $0.000$--$0.035$ for TV,
whereas at $I_0=100$ it produces at most $0.01$ for any type. At high noise the in-distribution prior therefore starts to create lesion-like structure as well as to erase it, although at a rate far below that under mismatch.
 
\emph{TV has a failure of its own.} For the smallest low-contrast lesion at $I_0=100$ the TV reconstruction contains $0.63$ false lesions per image (noise blobs), against $0.01$ for PnP. The statement that PnP creates more false lesions than TV under mismatch therefore applies to lesions of $\sigma_l\ge0.75$\,px, and under a matched prior is not supported at all.
 
Uncertainty is limited by the design: the error bars in Figure~\ref{fig:lesion-poisson} show the variation over three denoisers only, TV is a single run, and with about 170 valid lesions per cell the sampling error of a paired AUC is of the order of $\pm0.03$, so differences below that, such as the ties for the
largest lesions, do not separate the methods. A tercile analysis of PnP's paired AUC by lesion observability showed a decrease with observability on MNIST images and no trend on Fashion-MNIST images; since it depends on the image set it is
likely to reflect the background around the lesion rather than the operator, and we do not interpret it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Seven findings emerge from this study.
 
First, the learned prior's accuracy advantage over TV is conditional rather than universal. It is large and
significant at moderate noise and for moderate undersampling ($\approx+3.9$\,dB at $\sigma=0.05$ with 20--40 angles),
smaller at the extremes of both ranges, and in the near noise-free regime TV remains ahead by an amount that depends on
how well the data model matches the reconstruction model (Section~\ref{sec:crime-results}). Reporting a single aggregate
accuracy number for a learned reconstruction method without specifying the operating regime and the data model is
therefore misleading.
 
Second, PnP-PGD with an unconstrained CNN denoiser does not behave like a convergent optimization scheme: accuracy peaks
after a handful of iterations and then degrades while the data residual rises (Section~4.2). This is consistent with the
observation that convergence guarantees need constraints on the denoiser (e.g.\ non-expansiveness)~\cite{ryu2019pnp}. Our
decomposition adds a mechanism: with step $\eta=1/L$ the data step contracts the error in direction $i$ by
$(1-(s_i/s_1)^2)^K$, so after the few iterations selected by validation, directions with $s_i<0.3\,s_1$ are
barely corrected and the reconstruction there is largely the denoiser's. Iteration count is thus an implicit
regularization hyperparameter, and not an implementation detail.
 
Third, the reversal under distribution shift is a failure of the prior, not of the scheme. The matched-prior control
(Section~\ref{sec:seeds-results}) shows that PnP beats TV on Fashion-MNIST when the denoiser was trained on it, and the
error decomposition shows that the mismatched prior places up to three times the true energy in the weakly determined
subspace. The effect is robust: it exceeds seed-to-seed variation by an order of magnitude, persists under every
data model we tested, and cannot be removed by changing $\sigma_d$ or the iteration count. At the same time the
failure is not confined to the weakly determined subspace, so ``hallucination'' in the null-space sense is an
incomplete description.
 
Fourth, PSNR is not task performance. In distribution, the PSNR-tuned PnP reconstruction is about $4$\,dB better than TV yet
detects small inserted lesions markedly less reliably, because it attenuates them (Section~\ref{sec:lesion-results}). The
errors take two forms: erasure of unfamiliar small features when the prior is adequate, and invention of lesion-like
blobs when it is not. Re-tuning trades one against the other (Section~\ref{sec:oppoint-results}); only a matched prior leaves a
point that is competitive with TV on PSNR, detection and false structure at once, and operating points chosen in
distribution do not protect under shift.
 
Fifth, the learned method loses its advantage faster than TV under forward-model mismatch: it remains more accurate
throughout the tested range but its margin falls from $+3.85$\,dB to $+1.48$\,dB at a $4^\circ$ offset
(Section~4.3). This experiment was run with data generated by the nominal operator and should be repeated on finer-grid data.
 
Sixth, evaluation protocol matters. The inverse crime changes the low-noise PnP--TV gap by up to 5.5\,dB; a single denoiser
instance would have reported anywhere between $-2.83$ and $-3.43$\,dB for Fashion-MNIST depending on the seed; and operating
points for the lesion task were therefore chosen on held-out images rather than on the evaluation images, to avoid overstating the
benefit of re-tuning. These
results are consistent with broader findings on instabilities in learned reconstruction~\cite{antun2020instabilities,darestani2021shift} and
support the view that in-distribution accuracy alone is an insufficient basis for adopting a learned prior; mismatch,
shift, seed and task-based evaluation should be a standard part of such studies, not an optional addendum.
 
Seventh, the conclusions do not depend on the two simplifications of the early experiments. Replacing the dense
matrix by a matrix-free projector with an exact adjoint changes no reconstruction beyond $3\times10^{-4}$, and replacing additive Gaussian noise by Poisson
noise leaves the picture unchanged: the learned prior's gain over TV peaks at the same effective noise level and is lost under shift, the lesion deficit
of the PSNR-tuned prior in distribution is the same, and invention under mismatch is the same. The one new effect, a small rate of in-distribution false lesions at high noise, is a reminder that
the failure mode of a learned prior moves with the noise level, so conclusions about hallucination should be stated for an operating regime. The weighted Poisson data term did not help, so the Gaussian approximation is adequate in this setting.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a controlled, small-scale comparison of classical and learned regularization for sparse-view CT reconstruction under
noise, angular undersampling, forward-model mismatch, distribution shift and a lesion-detection task. The learned
Plug-and-Play prior outperforms TV by up to $\approx4$\,dB at moderate-to-high noise and for 5--90 angles, but the advantage is
conditional: the near noise-free ranking depends on the data model, the margin narrows under forward-model mismatch, and it reverses
under distribution shift. The reversal is caused by a mismatched prior rather than by the PnP scheme: a prior trained on the shifted
distribution beats TV on it, and the mismatched prior fills the weakly determined subspace with up to three times the true energy. In
a task-based evaluation, the PSNR-tuned PnP reconstruction attenuates small lesions in distribution and invents lesion-like blobs under
mismatch; re-tuning recovers TV-level detection only by giving up the PSNR advantage or by increasing false structure, except for a matched
prior. The iteration used here lacks a stable, accurate fixed point, so early stopping acts as a regularizer. Future work should extend
this study to larger, clinical-resolution data such as LoDoPaB-CT~\cite{leuschner2021} with patient-disjoint splits, patient-disjoint splits and clinical dose and geometry shifts (the matrix-free operator and Poisson model are now in place), and to provably convergent PnP variants and learned priors trained on diverse anatomy.
 

\section*{Limitations}
The study is intentionally small (28$\times$28 MNIST-derived images) so that a thorough experimental grid is possible; trends
may not transfer to natural-image or clinical CT resolution and geometry. Experiments E1--E5 use data generated with the
reconstruction operator (an inverse crime); the crime-free experiments E6--E10 use bicubic finer-grid data, and the sensitivity band
of Table~\ref{tab:crime} shows that the near noise-free ranking depends on this choice. The convergence study (E5) and the
forward-model mismatch study (E3) were not repeated on finer-grid data. Seed variation is estimated from three denoisers per
configuration, which gives only a crude standard deviation, and the Shepp--Logan test set is a single image with 20 noise draws, so its
intervals reflect noise rather than image variation. The error-decomposition statistics cannot separate prior-driven structure from
amplified noise except in comparisons with identical noise and settings, and the observability analysis of lesions is
confounded with lesion size and location. The lesion study uses bright additive Gaussian blobs, a location-known observer and a
false-lesion threshold that is a modelling choice, so false-lesion rates are meaningful for comparing methods but not as absolute rates;
the operating-point study uses one denoiser per prior, two lesion types and a coarse grid. Only two training distributions were
compared, so the explanation of the asymmetric transfer between them remains a hypothesis. The PnP scheme has no convergence guarantee,
and only moderate forward-model mismatches (up to $4^\circ$) were evaluated.
 The Poisson study uses a single attenuation scale ($\mu=2$), which keeps the signal dependence of the noise mild, a single angle count,
and one MNIST-trained denoiser in the dose sweep, so the shift results of E12 carry no seed uncertainty; its Shepp--Logan result is one image and one noise draw per dose.
The lesion study under Poisson noise inherits the limitations of E9, its TV baseline is a single run, and its observability analysis is exploratory and image-set dependent.
The matrix-free operator was validated only against the dense operator at $28\times28$, not at clinical size, where the cost of the matrix-free TV solver will have to be reduced.
